# The Rock × Funk Translation: From Cold Sweat to Dream Police
*Anchored at The Rolling Stones · Miss You · 1978*

**This playlist is**: a genealogy of the moment rock musicians began translating funk and disco's genetic material without fully becoming those genres — two layers audibly present, neither dissolved into the other. From James Brown's rhythmic foundation in 1967 to Mk.gee's guitar textures in 2024, the same translation logic recurs across generations.

## Track 1: James Brown — Cold Sweat (1967)
*From Cold Sweat / Funky Drummer era (1967–1970)*

**Root ancestor — M2 visit.** "Cold Sweat" is the genetic ground zero: James Brown strips harmony to a minimum and centers the groove entirely on the drum/bass interlock, establishing the percussive vocabulary that flows downward through disco, hip-hop, and Miss You.

Drummer Clyde Stubblefield joined Brown in 1965 and became the engine of the funk era; Questlove later called him "the one who defined funk music." "Funky Drummer" (1970), recorded the same era, contains a 20-second Stubblefield break that has been sampled on over 1,300 songs — the single most-sampled drum performance in music history.

**M2**: confirmed ancestor — one visit. The JBs rhythm section is the direct biological donor of every groove in this playlist.

## Track 2: Sly & the Family Stone — Family Affair (1971)
*From There's a Riot Goin' On*

**Drum-machine ancestor — M2/M3.** Sly Stone's pivot from collective euphoria to solitary drum-machine density on *Riot* is the first instance of the translation aesthetic being executed by one person playing all the parts — a structural precedent for Prince, Kevin Parker, and Mk.gee.

Stone used the Maestro Rhythm King MRK-2 drum machine on "Family Affair," manually overdubbing its sounds rather than using presets to create a deliberately dense, claustrophobic mix — one of the earliest hit recordings to prominently feature a drum machine, predating its widespread adoption by nearly a decade. He enlisted Billy Preston, Ike Turner, and Bobby Womack instead of his bandmates, marking the end of the band's communal creative model.

**M2/M3**: ancestor visit plus member-period attention — the single-artist total-control model pioneered here runs directly through Miss You's Billy Preston demo chain.

## Track 3: David Bowie — Fame (1975)
*From Young Americans*

**White-rock-translates-Black-music template — M1.** Bowie's "plastic soul" pivot at Sigma Sound in Philadelphia — recording with Black session musicians and calling the result calculated — establishes the exact template the Rolling Stones would execute three years later with disco. Same move, different genre.

John Lennon co-wrote "Fame" (with guitarist Carlos Alomar) at Electric Lady Studios in New York; it became Bowie's first US #1 single. Bowie's self-description of Young Americans as "plastic soul" is the most honest characterisation of the translation aesthetic: high craft, acknowledged artificiality, two lineages audibly present.

**M1**: blue-eyed soul is the direct aesthetic ancestor of the rock × disco translation — Bowie's Sigma Sound immersion in 1974 is three years upstream of Jagger's Studio 54 immersion.

## Track 4: Parliament — Give Up the Funk (Tear the Roof off the Sucker) (1976)
*From Mothership Connection*

**Maximalist funk peak — M2 visit.** Parliament's cosmic P-Funk mythology runs in parallel to disco's hedonism as the dominant Black American dance-music aesthetic of the mid-1970s — the groove infrastructure that hip-hop would later sample wholesale, and the direct living context against which Miss You positioned itself.

George Clinton assembled an enormous rotating collective that included Bootsy Collins on bass and Bernie Worrell on keyboards — and crucially Maceo Parker and Fred Wesley on horns, both poached from James Brown's JBs, creating a direct human lineage from Brown's Cold Sweat era to Parliament's peak. "Give Up the Funk" became Parliament's first million-selling single.

**M2**: confirmed ancestor — one visit. Parliament is the contemporaneous Black American dance-music peak that Miss You's rock × disco translation was engaging and partially approximating.

## Track 5: Bee Gees — Stayin' Alive (1977)
*From Saturday Night Fever*

**Disco apex — M2 context.** Saturday Night Fever (released December 1977) defined the disco moment that Miss You was recorded into; the Bee Gees' falsetto-driven four-on-the-floor anthems are the dominant commercial sound of the cultural space Stones were translating from a rock perspective. Stones were operating within this disco peak, not specifically targeting the Bee Gees.

"Stayin' Alive" runs at approximately 103 BPM — now used in CPR medical training. The Saturday Night Fever soundtrack songs were written with no idea they were making a soundtrack; Robert Stigwood commissioned them for the film after the fact. The SNF singles dominated US #1 for much of early 1978 ("Stayin' Alive" 4 weeks from February, "Night Fever" 8 weeks from March), and Andy Gibb — Bee Gees' younger brother — extended the family's reign with "Shadow Dancing" (7 weeks from June 17). It was Andy Gibb's "Shadow Dancing" that Miss You knocked off #1 on 5 August 1978, completing the Stones' entrance into a chart landscape entirely shaped by the Gibb brothers.

The "Stayin' Alive" connection becomes specifically audible later in this playlist: Blondie's drummer Clem Burke (Track 7) cites "Stayin' Alive" as the direct rhythmic model for the Heart of Glass backbeat.

**M2**: lateral context visit — the Bee Gees represent mainstream disco at its commercial apex; Miss You enters this Gibb-dominated chart landscape and displaces it.

## Track 6: The Rolling Stones — Miss You (1978)
*From Some Girls*

**ANCHOR.** The canonical case of a major rock band performing disco's genetic material under rock's stylistic envelope — and the map's centre of gravity. Everything before this track flows toward it; everything after flows from it.

Recorded during Keith Richards' drug-trial distraction, Mick Jagger took unusual artistic control. The four-on-the-floor pulse came from Billy Preston demoing it to Jagger at El Mocambo, Toronto, March 1977. The harmonica player is Sugar Blue (James Whiting), a 22-year-old American street musician discovered by Sandy Whitelaw busking in the Paris Métro. Keith's resistance to disco is documented; he later said the song "was a damn good disco record; it was calculated to be one." Bob Clearmountain mixed the 12-inch (8+ minutes) — the Stones' first.

**M1 + M3**: rock × disco translation aesthetic in full operation; member-period tension (Jagger's control during Keith's trial) is the human story behind the translation.

## Track 7: Blondie — Heart of Glass (1978)
*From Parallel Lines*

**Punk-scene translation — M1.** Where the Stones entered disco from classic rock, Blondie entered from the opposite direction — CBGB punk — and arrived at the same translation aesthetic in the same calendar year. Two starting points, one move.

Debbie Harry and Chris Stein had an embryonic version called "Once I Had a Love" (informally "The Disco Song") as early as 1974–75, but it resisted every format — ballad, reggae — until producer Mike Chapman pushed for a full disco treatment that the rest of the band, with their punk identities, found uncomfortable. Chris Stein and keyboardist Jimmy Destri purchased a Roland CR-78 rhythm machine from a store on Manhattan's 47th Street and brought it into the Record Plant session in June 1978 — the same year the device launched. Drummer Clem Burke, initially wary of the disco-leaning approach, took his backbeat directly from the Bee Gees' "Stayin' Alive" — making Track 5's rhythmic DNA literally audible in this song. The CR-78 also sent a trigger pulse to the synths, creating the stuttering keyboard motif central to the production.

**M1**: NYC punk × disco translation running in exact parallel to Miss You; the band's internal discomfort mirrors Keith Richards' resistance — same aesthetic tension, same year.

## Track 8: Devo — Jocko Homo (1978)
*From Q: Are We Not Men? A: We Are Devo!*

**Contrastive sibling — the road not taken.** Devo represents the explicit ideological inversion: released the same month Miss You was charting, their de-evolution theory holds that mass culture — including disco's hedonism and rock's spectacle — was evidence of human regression. They show what refusing the translation looks like.

Mark Mothersbaugh and Gerald Casale developed their de-evolution philosophy at Kent State University, where Casale witnessed the 1970 National Guard shootings of four classmates — giving Devo's reaction against mass-cultural pleasure a specific political and traumatic origin rather than mere aesthetic snobbery. Brian Eno was brought in to produce after Bowie was unavailable; the collaboration was contentious. Devo rejected most of Eno's synthesizer suggestions, preferring their rigidly pre-planned demo versions.

**M1 (inverted)**: new wave × industrial punk as explicit counter-program — the same post-punk toolkit used to refuse rather than enact the disco translation.

## Track 9: Chic — Good Times (1979)
*From Risque*

**Groove architecture — M1.** Nile Rodgers and Bernard Edwards architected the sonic vocabulary of Studio 54's peak; their locked rhythm-guitar-and-bass interplay defines how disco sounded at its most refined. Chic is also the direct human hinge between this playlist and Track 15 (Daft Punk).

The "Good Times" bassline was written the morning of recording: Edwards arrived late, Rodgers started screaming "Walk!" over the drums, and Edwards walked — the bassline was improvised in the session. Edwards' Fender bass line was recreated note-for-note by The Sugarhill Gang for "Rapper's Delight" (1979), launching commercial hip-hop and requiring a legal settlement that gave Rodgers and Edwards co-writing credit.

**M1**: Chic is the precision-engineered disco that Miss You was translating into rock — and Nile Rodgers' guitar is the specific thread that runs forward to Track 13 (Queen) and Track 15 (Daft Punk).

## Track 10: Joy Division — Isolation (1980)
*From Closer*

**Cold-wave inversion — M4.** Joy Division stands at the same 1978–1980 post-punk fork as Miss You and chose the opposite direction: cold electronic minimalism instead of rock-to-disco translation. The same cultural pressures and the same toolkit yield diametrically opposed results.

Peter Hook's melodic bass lines occupied the register conventionally held by guitar, inverting the rock × funk formula: where funk centralises bass rhythm, Joy Division centralised bass melody and emptied the rhythm of its body. Closer was produced by Martin Hannett with extreme spatial separation and electronic percussion replacing organic drums — the sonic opposite of disco's compression and density, yet both responded to the same post-punk moment.

**M4**: frequency hollowing — the systematic removal of sonic mass that funk and disco normally fill; Isolation shows the negative space left when you refuse the translation.

## Track 11: Talking Heads — Once in a Lifetime (1980)
*From Remain in Light*

**Intellectual translation — M1/M4.** David Byrne and Brian Eno used Fela Kuti's Afrobeat as template, building Remain in Light from layered drum-loop jams rather than composed songs — arriving at the same funk-polyrhythm territory as Miss You through a deliberately intellectual, studio-as-instrument path.

Recording started at Compass Point Studios in July 1980. Rather than writing songs around Byrne's lyrics in advance, the band jammed on looping grooves that Eno then layered with overdubs — a method Byrne described as sharing "that ecstatic communal feeling" of African music. The approach generated internal friction: "Once in a Lifetime" was the product of a process some members found incompatible with conventional songwriting.

**M1 + M4**: art-rock × Afrobeat polyrhythm translation; the drum-loop-first working method is the direct methodological ancestor of Khruangbin's practice on Track 17.

## Track 12: Queen — Another One Bites the Dust (1980)
*From The Game*

**Direct Chic transmission — M1/M3.** John Deacon translated direct studio exposure to Chic into a bass-driven funk groove two years after Miss You, demonstrating how the Chic aesthetic radiated outward to reshape rock bands across the Atlantic — making the creative transmission unusually traceable.

Bernard Edwards told NME that the song came about because Deacon spent time hanging out at Chic's studio; Nile Rodgers confirmed Deacon was sitting beside him during the "Good Times" recording session — the creative inheritance is personal and direct. Deacon wrote and largely tracked the song himself — bass, piano, electric guitar, and handclaps — while drummer Roger Taylor actively disliked it. Not planned as a single until Michael Jackson personally urged Deacon to release it.

**M1 + M3**: rock × funk translation with Chic as explicit source material; Deacon's creative control against band resistance mirrors the Jagger/Keith dynamic at the Miss You sessions.

## Track 13: Prince — When Doves Cry (1984)
*From Purple Rain*

**Frequency hollowing — M4.** Prince internalised the rock × funk translation so completely that it dissolved into a singular multiinstrumentalist idiom; "When Doves Cry" then pushed the formula to its extreme by removing the bass line entirely — making the absence of funk's foundational frequency the radical gesture.

Prince played all instruments himself, and after completing the track with all conventional elements including a bass line, he removed it entirely — reportedly telling his engineer "Ain't nobody gonna believe I'd do this." The final mix features a Linn LM-1 drum machine, a baroque synthesiser solo recorded at half speed then sped up, and no bass whatsoever. The bass removal created a mid-frequency hollowness not present in any prior funk or rock record.

**M4**: the definitive frequency hollowing event in the lineage — where Joy Division empties rhythm, Prince empties bass; both demonstrate translation can be enacted by subtraction.

## Track 14: Red Hot Chili Peppers — Give It Away (1991)
*From Blood Sugar Sex Magik*

**Lineage carrier — M1.** RHCP carry the rock × funk translation into the 1990s alternative explosion; Flea's Parliament/James Brown-schooled bass vocabulary fused with punk and hard-rock guitar makes Blood Sugar Sex Magik the track that brings the ancestry of Tracks 1–4 into the mainstream grunge era.

Flea has cited James Brown, Parliament-Funkadelic, and Bootsy Collins as primary bass influences — the same funk genealogy that fed into Miss You. The band lived together in producer Rick Rubin's Laurel Canyon mansion during recording. Flea's slap-and-pop technique on "Give It Away" is directly descended from Bootsy Collins's work with James Brown (1969–1971) — the same source material that influenced Bill Wyman's Miss You bassline research.

**M1**: rock × funk translation carried through 13 years; Flea's bass is the living connective tissue between James Brown's JBs and the 1990s alt-rock mainstream.

## Track 15: Daft Punk — Get Lucky (2013)
*From Random Access Memories*

**Explicit lineage closure — M1.** Daft Punk recruited Nile Rodgers — Chic's co-founder and the guitarist whose rhythm playing defined the disco that the Rolling Stones translated on Miss You — to perform on "Get Lucky," making the descent line from Track 9 audible and intentional across 34 years.

Daft Punk first met Nile Rodgers at a 1997 New York listening party for their debut album Homework; the RAM collaboration was therefore a reunion spanning 16 years of mutual respect. Rodgers played his 1959 "Hitmaker" Fender Stratocaster — the same guitar he used on Chic recordings in the 1977–1979 period. The track features live drums by Omar Hakim, who also played on Bowie's "Let's Dance," creating a direct personnel overlap with Track 3.

**M1**: electronic × funk translation; the Chic→Miss You→Get Lucky arc is explicit — Rodgers' Stratocaster is the same instrument, same idiom, 34 years on.

## Track 16: Tame Impala — The Less I Know the Better (2015)
*From Currents*

**Cyclical translation — M1.** Kevin Parker executes the psychedelic-to-disco pivot across the entire Currents album 37 years after Miss You, demonstrating that the rock × disco translation aesthetic is cyclically renewable — and explicitly traceable back to the same Bee Gees source material that surrounded the Stones in 1978.

Parker told The Guardian in 2015 that Currents' direction was crystallised by a specific moment: listening to the Bee Gees' "Stayin' Alive" while on mushrooms, riding around Los Angeles in a friend's old sedan at the end of the Lonerism tour. The experience "had the most profound emotional effect" and pushed him toward dance-floor music. "The Less I Know the Better" — the album's biggest single and Tame Impala's highest-selling song to date — emerged from this broader Currents direction rather than from a song-specific origin story. Like Prince and Sly Stone before him, Parker wrote, recorded, performed, and produced Currents entirely alone in his Perth home studio, enabling an undiluted translation without compromise.

**M1**: psychedelic rock × disco translation 37 years downstream; the Bee Gees citation connects Track 5 directly to Track 16, closing a temporal loop.

## Track 17: Khruangbin — María También (2018)
*From Con Todo el Mundo*

**Global extension — M4/M1.** Khruangbin operationalises the Remain in Light-descended working method — drum loop foundation first, bass overdub second, guitar last — and applies it to non-Western funk from Iran, Thailand, and the Mediterranean, demonstrating the translation aesthetic works across any global source material.

The trio (Laura Lee on bass, Mark Speer on guitar, Donald Ray Williams Jr. on drums) works from a self-described hierarchy: DJ's drum foundation first, then Laura Lee's bass, then Mark's guitar — the inverse of conventional rock recording and a direct inheritance from Brian Eno's Remain in Light drum-loop method. Con Todo el Mundo was recorded at Lincoln Manor, a converted Texas barn, and draws from 1970s Iranian funk and soul.

**M4**: frequency hollowing applied globally — Khruangbin compresses the two-instrument funk engine into one bass line, leaving Speer's guitar floating above like a displaced lead vocalist; M1 applies because the rock and non-Western funk layers remain audibly distinct.

## Track 18: Mk.gee — You Dreamed of Me (2024)
*From Two Star & the Dream Police*

**Living descendant — M1.** Michael Gordon's Two Star & the Dream Police deploys guitar textures in the tradition of early-1980s post-punk funk — citing Genesis, Lindsay Buckingham, and Prince as touchstones — and ranks as the most contemporary active confirmation that the rock × R&B/funk translation methodology remains productive 57 years after Cold Sweat.

Two Star & the Dream Police was released February 9, 2024 and critics drew explicit connections to Prince, Lindsay Buckingham, and Genesis — a citation chain running directly through the 1978–1984 period when the rock × funk synthesis was first fully articulated. Like Prince, Kevin Parker, and Sly Stone before him, Mk.gee is a sole-author, total-control studio project, enabling undiluted execution of the translation aesthetic. The album ranked #1 on year-end lists from Clash, Dazed, and The New York Times.

**M1**: the translation aesthetic at its newest living edge — same two-layer logic (rock envelope, R&B/funk genetic material), same single-author studio method, 2024.

# Miss You · 第 1 期 · bassline DNA 谱系
*Anchored at The Rolling Stones · Miss You · 1978*

**This episode is**: a 50-year genealogy of the bassline as a primary harmonic and rhythmic instrument, traced from James Brown's drum/bass interlock in 1967 through disco, post-punk, funk-rock, nu-disco, and global groove, arriving at Khruangbin's minimalist bass-first recording doctrine in 2018. Each track is selected for what it contributes to that low-frequency lineage — who inherited a bass philosophy, who transmitted it forward, who inverted it. Mk.gee at position 18 is a living coda: solo bedroom production with a programmed bass spine, the same logic still running in 2024.

*From Cold Sweat / Funky Drummer era (1967–1970)*

"Cold Sweat" is the genetic ground zero. James Brown strips harmony to a minimum and centers the groove entirely on the drum/bass interlock — establishing the percussive vocabulary that flows downward through disco, hip-hop, and Miss You. The bass is not decoration here; it is the harmonic statement.

Drummer Clyde Stubblefield joined Brown in 1965 and became the engine of the funk era. Questlove later called him "the one who defined funk music." "Funky Drummer" (1970), recorded during the same era, contains a 20-second Stubblefield break sampled on over 1,300 songs — the single most-sampled drum performance in music history. That break exists because the drum and bass were already locked so tightly that lifting either element intact still told the whole rhythmic story.

The JBs rhythm section — Stubblefield on drums, later Bootsy Collins on bass — is the direct biological donor of every groove in this playlist. What flows forward from here is not a stylistic influence; it is personnel, philosophy, and a specific low-frequency grammar.

## Track 2: Sly & the Family Stone — Family Affair (1971)
*From There's a Riot Goin' On*

**Genre**: Funk / Soul
**Bass role**: The drum-machine ancestor — first major hit to let a machine replace organic drums while bass carries the groove, establishing the single-person total-control bass model
**Connections in this episode**:

Sly Stone's pivot on *There's a Riot Goin' On* is the first instance of the translation aesthetic being executed by one person playing all the parts. He used the Maestro Rhythm King MRK-2 drum machine on "Family Affair," manually overdubbing its sounds rather than using presets — one of the earliest hit recordings to prominently feature a drum machine, predating its widespread adoption by nearly a decade.

The bass groove on "Family Affair" does not fight the machine; it locks to it. This is the new priority: the machine defines the pulse, the bass fills the space below it. Stone enlisted Billy Preston, Ike Turner, and Bobby Womack instead of his bandmates — a signal that the bass-forward, one-person-in-control model was a deliberate artistic choice, not a compromise.

That Billy Preston connection is structural to this playlist. Preston moved in Sly's orbit during this period, and it was Preston who would later demo the four-on-the-floor pulse to Mick Jagger in Toronto — carrying the bass philosophy from Sly's Riot sessions into Miss You's rhythm framework.

Bowie's "plastic soul" pivot at Sigma Sound in Philadelphia — recording with Black session musicians and naming the result calculated — establishes the exact bass-borrowing template the Rolling Stones would execute three years later. Bowie's self-description of Young Americans as "plastic soul" is the most honest characterization of the translation aesthetic: high craft, acknowledged artificiality, two musical lineages audibly present. The bass on "Fame" — recorded in Philadelphia with musicians rooted in the same soul and funk tradition Bowie was consciously borrowing — does not pretend to belong naturally; it announces its translation status.

What matters for this playlist's bass genealogy is not that Bowie "influenced" the Stones — it is that the move Bowie names and performs in 1975 is structurally identical to what Jagger does in 1978. Same vocabulary acquisition, same transparent translation, different scene.

George Clinton assembled a rotating collective that included Bootsy Collins on bass and Bernie Worrell on keyboards — and crucially Maceo Parker and Fred Wesley on horns, both recruited from James Brown's JBs. Recorded during Keith Richards' drug-trial distraction, Mick Jagger took unusual artistic control. Debbie Harry and Chris Stein had an embryonic version called "Once I Had a Love" (informally "The Disco Song") as early as 1974–75, but it resisted every format until producer Mike Chapman pushed for a full disco treatment. Chris Stein and keyboardist Jimmy Destri purchased a Roland CR-78 rhythm machine from a store on Manhattan's 47th Street and brought it into the Record Plant session in June 1978 — the same year the device launched.

The CR-78 sent a trigger pulse to the synths, creating the stuttering keyboard motif, but its primary structural role was rhythmic: it established the pulse that the bass locked onto. Drummer Clem Burke took his backbeat directly from the Bee Gees' "Stayin' Alive" — making the disco bass grammar literally audible in how the kit was played. The whole arrangement grew from the machine pulse outward, bass first.

Blondie's internal discomfort with the disco direction — Stein and Burke both had punk identities — mirrors Keith Richards' resistance to Miss You. Same translation, same friction, same year. The bass-machine interlock is the constant; everything else is the band's stylistic envelope.

## Track 9: Chic — Good Times (1979)
*From Risque*

**Genre**: Disco
**Bass role**: The precision-engineered disco bassline — Bernard Edwards' Fender bass line is the most-copied single bass phrase of the era, directly transmitted to Queen and indirectly to hip-hop; the locked guitar-bass interplay is the disco refinement of the JBs groove doctrine
**Connections in this episode**:

- ← Bee Gees — Stayin' Alive (1977): Chic inherits the front-line bass positioning from disco's commercial peak and makes it colder, more geometric, less euphoric

**Red-heart resonance**: _to be filled by build-exhibition-json.ts at runtime_

The "Good Times" bassline was written the morning of recording: Bernard Edwards arrived late, Nile Rodgers started calling out "Walk!" over the drums, and Edwards improvised the bass line in the session. That improvised line — locked in on the spot — has since been recreated note-for-note by The Sugarhill Gang for "Rapper's Delight" (1979), launching commercial hip-hop and requiring a legal settlement giving Rodgers and Edwards co-writing credit.

Edwards played a Fender bass through a direct box, routed clean into the console — no distortion, no coloring, just precision. The bass and Rodgers' rhythm guitar lock so tightly that lifting either element produces the full rhythmic statement. This is the JBs drum/bass interlock philosophy applied to disco's more refined studio context: two instruments, one groove, one logic.

Chic represents the apex of what Miss You was borrowing from. Rodgers and Edwards built their locked rhythm-guitar-and-bass interplay into a specific sonic architecture that Studio 54 ran on — and that architecture is what every track after this one is either transmitting forward, borrowing, or responding to.

## Track 10: Joy Division — Isolation (1980)
*From Closer*

**Genre**: Post-punk
**Bass role**: The cold-wave inversion — Peter Hook moves bass into the high melodic register conventionally held by guitar, emptying the low-frequency space that funk and disco normally fill; the negative image of the groove-centered bass
**Connections in this episode**:

- → Tame Impala — The Less I Know the Better (2015): Peter Hook's high-register melodic bass — bass occupying guitar's conventional register — recurs in Kevin Parker's psychedelic pop, where the melodic bass hook is the track's primary ear-catching element
**Red-heart resonance**: _to be filled by build-exhibition-json.ts at runtime_

Peter Hook's melodic bass lines on Closer occupy the register conventionally held by guitar, inverting the rock × funk formula: where funk centralizes bass rhythm, Joy Division centralizes bass melody and empties the rhythm of its body. Hook played through guitar amplifiers rather than bass rigs, pushing his bass into the upper-mid frequencies where guitars live — a deliberate gear choice that repositioned what bass could be.

Closer was produced by Martin Hannett with extreme spatial separation and electronic percussion replacing organic drums. The bass is the warmest, most melodic element in a deliberately cold sonic architecture. This is the frequency-hollowing thesis applied to the bass itself: rather than filling the low end, Hook evacuates it and moves the bass energy upward.

The relevance to this playlist's bass genealogy is structural: Joy Division demonstrates that moving bass out of its conventional register — whether upward into melody (Hook) or completely out of the mix (Prince) — is as significant a bass decision as any groove or interlock. The absence or displacement of bass frequency is itself a bass statement.

## Track 11: Talking Heads — Once in a Lifetime (1980)
*From Remain in Light*

**Genre**: Art rock / post-punk funk
**Bass role**: The drum-loop-first methodology — Tina Weymouth's clean, melodic Fender bass builds onto a pre-existing drum loop foundation; bass as the second layer after rhythm, not after harmony; direct methodological ancestor of Khruangbin's recording protocol
**Connections in this episode**:
- ← Sly & the Family Stone — Family Affair (1971): rhythm first, bass second — the structural priority of Sly's Maestro Rhythm King sessions is codified by Eno into an album-wide method on Remain in Light
- ← Blondie — Heart of Glass (1978): the CR-78-drives-bass-groove logic that Blondie used in New York is expanded into a full compositional system; Eno turns the drum-loop-then-bass method into doctrine

- → Daft Punk — Get Lucky (2013): building rhythm first and melody second — the Remain in Light method — is reborn digitally in Random Access Memories; the structural priority is continuous
- → Khruangbin — María También (2018): Tina Weymouth's clean, melodic, spacious bass and the drum-loop-first recording protocol are the direct methodological ancestors of Laura Lee's bass-first approach in the Texas barn
**Red-heart resonance**: _to be filled by build-exhibition-json.ts at runtime_

Recording started at Compass Point Studios in July 1980. Rather than writing songs around Byrne's lyrics in advance, the band jammed on looping grooves that Eno then layered with overdubs — a method that produced internal friction but also produced "Once in a Lifetime." The bass was always the second layer: drum loop established the pulse, Weymouth's bass settled into the space below and around it, then melody and texture were added on top.

Tina Weymouth's bass on Remain in Light is clean, melodic, and spacious — Fender bass through minimal processing, notes chosen for their groove function rather than harmonic decoration. The Afrobeat influence (Fela Kuti's polyrhythmic approach, acknowledged by Byrne and Eno) arrives through the drum loop foundation; the bass translates that foundation into something recognizably Western and dance-floor legible.

This recording-order hierarchy — drums first, bass second, everything else after — is the structural link between Sly Stone's Riot-era sessions, Remain in Light, and Khruangbin's Lincoln Manor recordings. The bass doesn't compose; it responds to the drum pulse. That response is the groove.

Deacon wrote and largely tracked "Another One Bites the Dust" himself — bass, piano, electric guitar, and handclaps. Drummer Roger Taylor actively disliked the song. The bass on "Another One Bites the Dust" at stadium volume — the level this song was played at for the next forty years — is a low-frequency statement at a scale that Chic's disco context never required. ## Track 13: Prince — When Doves Cry (1984)
*From Purple Rain*

**Genre**: Minneapolis funk-rock
**Bass role**: The frequency hollowing event — Prince internalized the rock × funk bass synthesis so completely that he could remove the bass line entirely; the absence of bass as the radical gesture, the single most extreme bass decision in this lineage
**Connections in this episode**:
- ← Sly & the Family Stone — Family Affair (1971): the single-person total-control recording model — one person programming every sonic element — runs from Sly's Riot sessions directly to Prince, who is its most extreme practitioner

Prince played all instruments on "When Doves Cry" himself. After completing the track with all conventional elements including a bass line, he removed it entirely — reportedly telling his engineer "Ain't nobody gonna believe I'd do this." The final mix features a Linn LM-1 drum machine, a baroque synthesizer solo recorded at half speed then sped up, and no bass whatsoever.

The bass removal created a mid-frequency hollowness not present in any prior funk or rock record. The Linn LM-1 drum machine carries all the rhythmic low-frequency energy, and the guitar sits in the upper registers where the bass would normally anchor the arrangement. The result is a sound with no foundation in the conventional sense — a deliberate, architecturally aware subtraction.

This is the definitive frequency-hollowing event in this playlist's lineage. Joy Division moved bass upward into melody; Prince moved it out of the recording entirely. Both are extreme responses to the same bass grammar that James Brown's drum/bass interlock established in 1967. The distance from Cold Sweat to When Doves Cry is the full range of what a bass decision can mean.

# 基因穿外套: From the Lebrija Piano to the Olive-Field Cante
*Anchored at Dorantes · Orobroy · 1998 / 2010 (dual-version anchor)*

**This playlist is**: a genealogy of *translation aesthetic* (M1, "基因穿外套") — moments where one tradition speaks in another tradition's vocabulary and you can still hear both layers. Jazz-piano voicings under bulería compás. Cuban son under flamenco cante. Trap palmas under bulería palmas. Cante hondo over a children's choir.

The anchor sits in the middle of the listener's own five-year arc into Iberian music: from Buena Vista Social Club (2023) through Cuban jazz/funk (PALO!, Irakere, 2025) into deep flamenco (Dorantes, Lebrijano, Carmen Linares, David Lagos — late 2025 through May 2026). This map traces the lineage upstream and the descent downstream while keeping the listener's own ear in view.

**Total tracks**: 24 · **Estimated time**: ~105–115 min

## Track 1: Keith Jarrett — The Köln Concert, Part I (1975)
*From The Köln Concert*

**Solo-piano source dialect — M2 visit, upward root.** Before Orobroy is anything else, it is a particular sound: a sustained left-hand ostinato over which a singing right hand develops a long-form melisma. Jarrett's 1975 Köln Concert is the European jazz-piano template for that form — and the texture Spanish music press repeatedly compares Orobroy to.

Recorded 24 January 1975 at the Köln Opera House on a smaller, partially broken Bösendorfer baby grand mistakenly delivered for the show — Jarrett initially refused to play it, then worked around the broken upper register by emphasising mid-register ostinatos. Released later that year on ECM. Over four million copies sold. Jarrett's *Where Can I Go Without You* (from *Jasmine*) sits in the user's red-heart since 2023 — the listener already knows this dialect.

**M1**: jazz-piano vocabulary, prepared in Europe, ready to be imported into flamenco a generation later. **M2 / adjacent**: red-heart hit on the artist (different track, 2023-10-23).

## Track 2: Lole y Manuel — Nuevo Día (1975)
*From Nuevo Día*

**Nuevo flamenco genesis — M2.** The same year as Köln Concert, in Seville, a Romani cantaora and a hippie-leaning guitarist released the album most flamenco historians cite as the founding act of *nuevo flamenco* — softening cante's traditional pain into something more lyrical, smuggling Woodstock-era affect into Andalucía at a politically charged moment.

Released 1975 on Movieplay/Gong, produced by Ricardo Pachón (later the producer of Camarón's *Leyenda*). First album to feature a mellotron in flamenco. Released without promotion; became a long-tail critical and commercial success and the document Camarón's 1979 rupture builds on.

**M1**: flamenco × hippie folk × Andalusian-Arabic Mediterranean fusion — the upstream condition for everything that follows.

[Listen on NetEase — search Lole y Manuel · Nuevo Día]

## Track 3: Paco de Lucía — Entre Dos Aguas (1973)
*From Fuente y Caudal*

**Commercial canonization of modern flamenco — M2.** A flamenco rumba improvised under deadline pressure when producer José Torregrosa told Paco the album was incomplete. *Fuente y Caudal* sold 300,000 copies — the album that crossed over from specialist flamenco to mainstream Spanish listening. The first widely-heard flamenco recording with congas, bongos, and electric bass; replaced palmas with bongo on a flagship piece. The widened palette every node in this map inherits.

Both modernization and commercial root for everything Dorantes' generation built on. Chano Domínguez named Paco as one of his two principal influences; Vicente Amigo, who appears at Track 12, was inspired at age three watching Paco on television.

**M1**: solo-instrument flamenco virtuoso × Latin/jazz instrumentation — the public template.

[Listen on NetEase — search Paco de Lucía · Entre Dos Aguas]

## Track 4: Camarón de la Isla — La Leyenda del Tiempo (1979)
*From La Leyenda del Tiempo*

**Nuevo flamenco rupture — M2.** Camarón took Lorca's poetry and accepted electric guitar, drum kit, sitar, and flute (Jorge Pardo) into the cante. Initially scandalized purists and was a commercial failure on release; later canonized as the moment when "nuevo flamenco" became inevitable. Every translation aesthetic in this map descends from the rupture this album made acceptable — including Dorantes' 1998 move to put a piano at the centre of flamenco compás.

Released 16 June 1979 on PolyGram. Produced by Ricardo Pachón (also Lole y Manuel, Veneno, Imán). Tomatito at Camarón's side on guitar — the same Tomatito who appears at Track 11 as Michel Camilo's duo partner.

**M1 / M3**: cante jondo × rock × jazz — the founding document of modern Spanish translation aesthetic.

[Listen on NetEase — search Camarón · La Leyenda del Tiempo]

## Track 5: El Lebrijano — Persecución (1976)
*From Persecución*

**Cante hondo root, family voice predecessor — M3.** Dorantes' uncle. Same Peña-Perrate-Pinini-Bacán Romani flamenco dynasty of Lebrija and Utrera. *Persecución* (1976) narrates the persecution of the Romani people through poet Félix Grande's verses, breaking convention by introducing "cante por galeras" as a vehicle for long-form historical narrative. The album's modernity is conceptual / textual rather than instrumental — voice and guitar, but carrying poetic weight unfamiliar to traditional cante.

Born Juan Peña Fernández, 1941, Lebrija. Brother of Pedro Peña; cousin of Pedro Bacán; uncle of Dorantes (David Peña Dorantes). Lebrijano died in 2016. This is the predecessor pole of the family arc — the 2010 duet with his nephew Dorantes is encoded separately at Track 16 as the closure pole.

**M3**: member-period attention — the family voice on its own, before the duet.

[Listen on NetEase — search El Lebrijano · Persecución]

## Track 5b: Isaac Albéniz — Iberia (1909)
*From Iberia, Books I–IV (suite for piano)*

**Spanish classical-piano root — upward axis, blind spot.** Founding figure of Spanish musical nationalism. *Iberia* (1905–09) is a 12-piece programmatic suite painting Spanish regional portraits — Andalusian, Castilian, Catalan — using cante-jondo melismatic figuration and zapateado rhythmic cells processed through late-Romantic / proto-Impressionist piano-virtuoso harmony. The suite is itself a cartography of Spain: the gesture Dorantes inverts in 1998 when he carries flamenco materials *into* a piano-led harmonic vocabulary.

Dorantes' own RomArchive portrait describes him "reaching into the late romanticist harmony of Falla, Granados, and Albéniz" for his harmonic and compositional vocabulary. Trancoso's *El piano flamenco* doctoral thesis (hosted by the Lebrija municipal archive) names Albéniz/Falla as the precedent layer of the flamenco-piano tradition. Chano Domínguez's quartet project also explicitly explores the Falla / Albéniz / Granados / Mompou repertoire.

**M1**: cante-jondo material rendered through nationalist-classical harmony — the inverse gesture of Orobroy, sourced from a generation before recorded flamenco.

[Listen on NetEase — search Isaac Albéniz · Iberia]

## Track 6: Pata Negra — Blueslería / Blues de la Frontera (1987)
*From Blues de la Frontera*

**'Blueslería' precedent — M1.** The Amador brothers (Raimundo and Rafael) coined the genre name "blueslería" on this record — pure blues at the rhythm of bulerías. The exact gestural precedent for Dorantes' translation move eleven years later: foreign harmonic vocabulary inhabits flamenco's body without dissolving it. Released also in the UK, US, and Japan; the last record the brothers made together.

Released 1987 on Nuevos Medios. After this, Raimundo left the band; Rafael continued solo. Presented live in Paris and New York — among the first nuevo flamenco records to receive a substantial international release.

**M1**: flamenco × Delta blues × jazz/swing/reggae — the same gesture as Orobroy, slightly different coat.

[Listen on NetEase — search Pata Negra · Blues de la Frontera]

## Track 7: Chano Domínguez — Chano (1993)
*From Chano (or Flamenco Sketches, 2012)*

**Methodological elder brother — M1, blind spot.** The flamenco-piano predecessor whose method Dorantes' Orobroy steps into and reshapes. Domínguez (b. Cádiz, 1960) is openly the pianist who declared Bill Evans and Paco de Lucía as his two principal influences and built a piano language fluent in tangos, bulerías, alegrías, soleás. *Chano* (1993) and the later Blue Note album *Flamenco Sketches* (2012, Grammy-nominated) bracket the era. Same materials as Dorantes, slightly earlier in the timeline.

Self-described chain of influence: Mahavishnu Orchestra and Weather Report → Bill Evans and Thelonious Monk → Paco de Lucía. The Bill-Evans / Paco-de-Lucía pairing is the public methodological signature both pianists share.

**M1 (methodological_descent)**: flamenco-jazz piano as a project — Dorantes inherits and extends.

[Listen on NetEase — search Chano Domínguez · Hands]

## Track 8: Dorantes — Orobroy (1998 original)
*From Orobroy*

**ANCHOR (first version).** Solo piano. Wordless cante melisma over a long-form left-hand ostinato. Bulería compás carried by palmas and cajón. Caló-language title — *orobroy* means "my thought" in the language of the Spanish Romani. Released 1998 on BMG/RCA Spain as Dorantes' debut. Awarded Spain's Premio Nacional de la Crítica.

This is the bare version. The piano sits at the centre of flamenco for the first time at this scale. The 12-year self-translation that produces Track 14 is already implicit here, but the listener doesn't know it yet.

**M1 + M4**: jazz-piano voicings + bulería compás + cante-jondo melisma + Caló-language frame; sparse, deliberately incomplete frequency spectrum.

[Listen on NetEase — search Dorantes · Orobroy]

## Track 9: Diego El Cigala & Bebo Valdés — Lágrimas Negras (2003)
*From Lágrimas Negras*

**Lateral counterpart, transatlantic — M1, critical bridge to user's Buena Vista cluster.** The exact lateral counterpart to Orobroy's translation aesthetic, executed by another pair on the same circuit five years later. Cigala's flamenco voice in Bebo Valdés' Cuban piano body — Cigala sings boleros, tangos, coplas; Valdés' piano accompanies *flamenco* readings of standards like "La bien pagá" and the title track. Same translation logic, different language pair.

Released 20 August 2003 on Calle 54 Records / BMG. Produced by Spanish guitarist/producer Javier Limón (who also produces Buika at Track 16) and filmmaker Fernando Trueba. Bebo Valdés was 84 at recording, exiled from Cuba since 1960; Trueba's *Calle 54* documentary had returned him to Spanish-speaking visibility.

**M1**: flamenco cante × Cuban son — bridge to the user's pre-existing Cuban son ear.

[Listen on NetEase — search Cigala Bebo · Lágrimas Negras]

## Track 10: Buena Vista Social Club — Chan Chan (1997)
*From Buena Vista Social Club*

**Cuban son root — hit.** The 1997 Ry Cooder–Juan de Marcos González project that returned aging Cuban son players to international visibility. Recorded March 1996 at EGREM Studios, Havana, in seven days. Won 1998 Grammy for Best Tropical Latin Performance; over 8 million copies sold.

User's red-heart on this album spans 2023-08 through 2024-01 — the longest-running Latin-music thread in the listener's library, the pre-existing Cuban-son ear that *Lágrimas Negras* (Track 9) translates flamenco into. Compay Segundo, Ibrahim Ferrer, Rubén González, Eliades Ochoa — most over 70 at recording.

**M2 / hit**: confirmed lateral root; the listener already knows the language Cigala–Bebo translate flamenco into.

[Listen on NetEase](https://music.163.com/#/song?id=496869570) — Buena Vista Social Club · Chan Chan

## Track 11: Tomatito & Michel Camilo — Spain (2000)
*From Spain*

**Same-era dialogue — flamenco guitar × Latin-jazz piano.** The 1998 Barcelona International Jazz Festival improvisation that became this album — exact-year contemporary with *Orobroy* (1998). Tomatito (Camarón's longtime guitarist on *Leyenda*) and Dominican Latin-jazz pianist Michel Camilo improvising Chick Corea's "Spain" and "Bésame Mucho" at the Palau de la Música. *Spain* (2000) won Best Latin Jazz Album at the inaugural Latin Grammys.

Same translation logic Dorantes performs alone, here distributed across two virtuosi from two traditions. Followed by *Spain Again* (2006) and *Spain Forever* (2016) — a sustained translation collaboration.

**M1**: flamenco guitar × Latin-jazz piano dialogue.

[Listen on NetEase — search Tomatito Michel Camilo · Spain]

## Track 12: Vicente Amigo — Ciudad de las Ideas (2000)
*From Ciudad de las Ideas*

**Same-era guitar peer — M1.** Amigo (b. 1967) and Dorantes (b. 1969) are the same generation of post-Paco solo-instrument flamenco modernizers — Amigo on guitar, Dorantes on piano. *Ciudad de las Ideas* (2000) sits two years after *Orobroy* (1998); won the 2001 Latin Grammy for Best Flamenco Album and the 2002 Ondas Award. Often called "the natural continuation of Paco de Lucía."

Born Guadalcanal, raised in Córdoba; studied for a decade with Manolo Sanlúcar; performed with Paco de Lucía in *Leyendas de la guitarra*. The album's title track "Córdoba" is "a personal vision of my home, letting everyone know that this place is united with my heart."

**M1**: same modernization question, addressed from a different solo instrument.

[Listen on NetEase — search Vicente Amigo · Ciudad de las Ideas]

## Track 13: Esperanza Fernández — En el compás de su sangre (2001)
*From Esperanza Fernández (debut album)*

**Deep-cante female voice, family circuit — M1, blind spot.** Born 1966 in Triana, Seville. Daughter of cantaor Curro Fernández and Lebrija-born cantaora Pepa Vargas — and Esperanza's mother and Dorantes' father are second cousins. The collaboration is family. *En el compás de su sangre* is a canonical soleá with lyrics by José de la Tomasa, where Dorantes accompanies her on piano, improvising over a falseta during the central section. The album also includes the Romani anthem *Gelem, Gelem* with Dorantes on piano, and *Garrotín* with lyrics by José Saramago.

The deeper voice the user asked for. Where the previous draft of this map placed Niña Pastori (Camarón's San Fernando lineage, pop-leaning crossover) in this slot, Esperanza sits closer to the Orobroy core: same Sevillian-Romani circuit as the Peña-Perrate-Pinini-Bacán dynasty, deep soleá and seguiriya register, recorded duo with Dorantes himself. Debuted at sixteen with Mario Maya's *Amargo*; later shared stages with Paco de Lucía, Camarón, Enrique Morente, Rafael Riqueni; received the Giraldillo del Cante at the XV Bienal de Flamenco de Sevilla (2008).

**M1**: deep cante (female voice) × Dorantes piano — the duet form one tier removed from the anchor itself.

[Listen on NetEase — search Esperanza Fernández · En el compás de su sangre]

## Track 14: Dorantes — Orobroy (Nueva versión) (2010)
*From El tiempo por testigo... A Sevilla — **HIT**, red-heart 2025-11-10*

**ANCHOR returns — self-translation across 12 years.** The 2010 *Nueva versión* re-orchestrates the 1998 piece with a 28-voice children's choir from the Fundación Meridianos and 9 schools in Seville's Polígono Sur — a marginalised neighbourhood with a strong Romani population. Recorded live in studio with the working trio (Dorantes piano, Francis Posé double bass, Javi Ruibal percussion) on the album marking 20 years of the composer's career.

This is the version in the user's red-heart (2025-11-10). The 1998 → 2010 gap is itself a translation event: the song self-translating from solo to communal, from individual interiority to collective barrio. After hearing 13 stops of context, the listener can now hear what the choir adds and what it covers — the 1998 silence was already singing, the 2010 children make it audible.

**M1 (self-translation) + M3 (community choir as new participant)**: the inner mirror of the whole episode.

## Track 15: Dorantes & El Lebrijano — Agua, Aire y Fuego (2010)
*From El tiempo por testigo... A Sevilla — **HIT**, red-heart 2025-11-27*

**Family lineage made literal — hit, closure pole of the family arc.** From the same 2010 album as Track 14, with cantaor Nolasco also featured. Piano nephew accompanying cante-jondo uncle on a single track. Dorantes and El Lebrijano belong to the same Peña-Perrate-Pinini-Bacán Romani dynasty of Lebrija and Utrera; this duet is the genealogy of the entire upward axis made audible in one performance.

The 34-year arc from *Persecución* (Track 5, 1976) to this duet is the family voice translated across two generations: cante hondo as concept-album poetry → cante hondo as piano-accompanied family ballad. Lebrijano died in 2016, six years after this recording — *Agua, Aire y Fuego* is among the late performances of his cante. The user's red-heart on this track (2025-11-27) confirms the listener's ear has gone all the way to the family table.

**M3 (member-period attention) + hit**: the in-library closure signal that the upward axis has been heard.

## Track 16: Concha Buika — Mi Niña Lola (2006)
*From Mi Niña Lola*

**Female-voice diaspora translation — M1.** Born 11 May 1972, Palma de Mallorca, to parents from Equatorial Guinea (Bubi ethnic group). Buika translates *between* Black diaspora vocal traditions (jazz, soul, Black Cuban) and Andalusian copla / flamenco — sometimes within a single phrase. *Mi Niña Lola* (2006), produced by Javier Limón (the same producer as *Lágrimas Negras*, Track 9), is her flamenco-zambra-as-jazz-diva statement.

The producer link makes the lineage explicit: Limón's role connects Buika's project directly to the Cigala–Bebo translation circuit. The original *Mi Niña Lola* is a 1950s zambra; Buika recovers it 50 years later as if a jazz/soul diva were singing copla.

**M1**: flamenco × copla × jazz × soul, female-voice axis, post-Lágrimas Negras producer ecology.

[Listen on NetEase — search Buika · Mi Niña Lola]

## Track 17: Sílvia Pérez Cruz — No te pude conocer (2012)
*From 11 de Novembre*

**Female song-form translation — M1, methodologically descended.** The Catalan-rooted vocalist who sustains the *song-form* translation across folk, acoustic jazz, fado, flamenco, Cuban, Brazilian — sometimes within a single album, frequently across four languages (Catalan, Spanish, Portuguese, Galician). *11 de Novembre* (2012) is her first proper solo album of original songs.

Album titled after her late father Cástor Pérez's birthday (he died two days before it). Co-produced with Raül Fernández Refree. Methodologically descended from Buika's earlier diaspora-translation work, advanced one generation.

**M1 + M4**: female song-form translation across Iberian, Latin American, and Lusophone languages; sparse arrangements emphasizing voice texture.

## Track 18: Rosalía — Malamente (2018)
*From El Mal Querer*

**Gen Z flamenco re-frame — M1.** The canonical Gen Z flamenco re-frame. *El Mal Querer* was Rosalía's flamenco-studies thesis at ESMUC under José Miguel Vizcaya "Chiqui de la Línea," released as a major-label album. *Malamente* opens with a clear bulería compás laid as the foundation of trap palmas — the single most-cited example in the 2010s of flamenco vocabulary translated into pop electronics with both languages still audibly separable.

Released 2 November 2018 on Sony / Columbia, co-produced with El Guincho. Multiple Latin Grammys including Album of the Year — the first flamenco-rooted album to win in that category.

**M1**: bulería compás × trap kicks; same translation gesture as Orobroy, twenty years later, different surface.

[Listen on NetEase — search Rosalía · Malamente]

## Track 19: María José Llergo — Niña de las Dunas (2018)
*From Sanación*

**Current-generation cante — M1.** Born 1994, Pozoblanco, Córdoba. Learned cante from her grandfather Pepe (José Sánchez Muñoz) in the olive fields. Trained at conservatory (solfeo, violin) before flamenco specialization at ESMUC under Chiqui de la Línea — same teacher as Rosalía, four years apart. *Niña de las Dunas* (2018) was her first composition, written from feeling distant from her loved ones in Córdoba while studying in Barcelona.

Cited as one of the voices of the new generation of flamenco — deeper inherited cante line than Rosalía's, lighter electronic surface. Won the Goya Award for Best Original Song 2022 ("Te espera el mar," from the film *Mediterráneo*).

**M1**: cante hondo (inherited from grandfather) × contemporary songcraft — direct same-era dialogue with Rosalía.

[Listen on NetEase — search María José Llergo · Niña de las Dunas]

## Track 20: C. Tangana feat. Niño de Elche y La Húngara — Tú Me Dejaste De Querer (2020)
*From El Madrileño*

**Post-Rosalía mainstream — M1.** *El Madrileño* (2021) was the best-selling album in Spain that year and broke the post-Rosalía flamenco-pop export market into US Latin charts (#8 US Latin Albums). Twelve of fourteen tracks built around collaborations with flamenco / Latin folk / rock figures. *Tú Me Dejaste De Querer* — released as a single on 6 November 2020 — is the rumba-bachata hinge piece, with Niño de Elche (avant-flamenco) and La Húngara (rumba/copla).

Tangana described the song as "the most important song of his career." Released through Sony Music; co-produced with Alizzz.

**M1**: flamenco rumba × bachata × hip-hop, mainstream apex of the post-Rosalía pipeline.

## Track 21: Nathy Peluso — Buenos Aires (2020)

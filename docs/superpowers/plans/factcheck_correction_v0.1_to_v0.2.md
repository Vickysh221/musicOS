## EDIT 1 — Track 5 (Bee Gees): Fix the Billboard chart error

**Problem**: The annotation states "It was the SNF soundtrack that Miss You knocked off the top spot in August 1978". This is factually wrong. Miss You hit US #1 on 5 August 1978, ending the **seven-week reign of Andy Gibb's "Shadow Dancing"** — not the Saturday Night Fever soundtrack. The SNF soundtrack is an album and does not appear on the Hot 100 singles chart; the SNF singles ("Stayin' Alive", "Night Fever", "How Deep Is Your Love") had all dropped from #1 earlier in 1978.

Additionally, the playlist's framing — that the Bee Gees were the "specific disco target" Stones were "translating and competing with" — is over-stated. There is no documented evidence that Stones were specifically targeting the Bee Gees. The accurate framing is contextual: SNF was the cultural disco moment Stones were operating within, but "Stayin' Alive" specifically influenced **Clem Burke's drumming on Heart of Glass** (Track 7), which is the documented connection.

**Find this exact paragraph** in Track 5:

```
**Disco apex — M2 context.** The Saturday Night Fever soundtrack was occupying US #1 when Miss You was recorded and released; the Bee Gees' falsetto-driven four-on-the-floor anthems are the specific disco target the Stones were both translating and competing with.

"Stayin' Alive" runs at approximately 103 BPM — now used in CPR medical training. The Saturday Night Fever songs were written with no idea they were making a soundtrack; Robert Stigwood commissioned them for the film after the fact. It was the SNF soundtrack that Miss You knocked off the top spot in August 1978, making the Bee Gees and the Stones simultaneous protagonists of the same cultural collision.

**M2**: lateral context visit — the Bee Gees represent mainstream disco at its commercial apex, the specific moment Miss You enters and displaces.
```

**Replace with**:

```
**Disco apex — M2 context.** Saturday Night Fever (released December 1977) defined the disco moment that Miss You was recorded into; the Bee Gees' falsetto-driven four-on-the-floor anthems are the dominant commercial sound of the cultural space Stones were translating from a rock perspective. Stones were operating within this disco peak, not specifically targeting the Bee Gees.

"Stayin' Alive" runs at approximately 103 BPM — now used in CPR medical training. The Saturday Night Fever soundtrack songs were written with no idea they were making a soundtrack; Robert Stigwood commissioned them for the film after the fact. The SNF singles dominated US #1 for much of early 1978 ("Stayin' Alive" 4 weeks from February, "Night Fever" 8 weeks from March), and Andy Gibb — Bee Gees' younger brother — extended the family's reign with "Shadow Dancing" (7 weeks from June 17). It was Andy Gibb's "Shadow Dancing" that Miss You knocked off #1 on 5 August 1978, completing the Stones' entrance into a chart landscape entirely shaped by the Gibb brothers.

The "Stayin' Alive" connection becomes specifically audible later in this playlist: Blondie's drummer Clem Burke (Track 7) cites "Stayin' Alive" as the direct rhythmic model for the Heart of Glass backbeat.

**M2**: lateral context visit — the Bee Gees represent mainstream disco at its commercial apex; Miss You enters this Gibb-dominated chart landscape and displaces it.
```
